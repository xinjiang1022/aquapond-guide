Koi Bloating and Constipation: Belly Swelling, Feces and Feeding Adjustments

Koi Care~6 min readUpdated Nov 28, 2026

A rounded belly does not always mean a problem. Well-fed koi, females carrying eggs in season and fish that have eaten a large meal can all look full, while real constipation or illness tends to come with other changes in appetite, movement or droppings. The safest approach is to look at the whole picture — feeding record, water temperature, behavior and the fish's droppings — before changing diet or reaching for treatment.

The short answer

Feeding-related bloating usually traces to too much food, dry pellets that swell after eating, or digestion slowing in cool water. It often improves when you cut the ration, use an appropriate food for the season, and let the fish clear its system. Swelling that is one-sided, persistent, or paired with poor appetite, listlessness, clamped fins, redness or abnormal droppings may be a health issue rather than simple constipation.

RememberA swollen belly has several causes that look alike. Do not assume constipation and starve the fish for days; check water quality, season, behavior and droppings first, and get specialist advice if symptoms persist or worsen.

A calm check before changing anything

1. Look at the belly and the whole fish

Note whether the roundness is even or one-sided, and whether the fish swims upright and normally. Breeding females are often fuller around the belly in season, described in the male and female koi guide and the spawning season guide. A fish that sits low, loses condition or shows skin changes is a less reassuring sign; compare symptoms against common koi diseases.

2. Review the feeding record

Think back over the last few feeds: amount, frequency, pellet type and whether food was left uneaten. Overfeeding is the most common cause of digestive upset in hobby ponds. General feeding habits are covered in the koi feeding guide, timing in the best time to feed koi, and cool-weather ration changes in cold-water feeding. Crowding can also compete for food and add stress; see pond stocking density.

3. Check water temperature and quality

Cold water slows a koi's metabolism, so the same ration that suits summer can sit heavy in winter. Test ammonia, nitrite, pH and temperature, and confirm filtration and aeration are working. Poor water adds stress and can show up alongside appetite problems covered in why koi stop eating. Parameters are listed in the water parameters guide.

4. Watch the droppings and appetite

Normal, firm droppings suggest digestion is moving along. Floating, thin or stringy feces, little passing at all, or a belly that stays round after a day or two point toward a digestive issue. A fish that still feeds eagerly and swims normally is generally in better shape than one that hangs back or refuses food. Record what you observe so the pattern is clear over a day or two.

Feeding adjustments that may help

If the cause looks like overfeeding or mild digestive slowdown, reduce the ration to what the fish clear quickly, avoid feeding heavy meals in cold weather, and use a food matched to the water temperature. Some keepers briefly soak dry pellets or adjust the pellet size, but changes should be gentle and watched rather than repeated through multiple fasting days. Keep the water clean and let normal activity and filtration continue; do not introduce unfamiliar foods or treatments while you are still observing the cause.

When to seek specialist advice

Consult a reputable aquatic specialist or veterinarian if the swelling persists or grows, looks one-sided or fluid-filled, or comes with refusal to feed, listlessness, clamped fins, red or ulcerated skin, abnormal swelling across several fish, or difficulty holding balance. New arrivals should also be assessed in context; the quarantine guide for new koi explains why isolating and observing new fish matters before they join the main pond.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Starving the fish for days: Prolonged fasting adds stress; reduce ration and observe rather than withholding food indefinitely.
  • Calling every full belly illness: Breeding females and well-fed fish look rounded, especially in season.
  • Treating without a water test: Bad water and stress can look like digestive trouble and must be ruled out first.
  • Overfeeding in cold weather: Slow digestion in cool water makes a summer-sized ration too heavy.
