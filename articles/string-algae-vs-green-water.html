String Algae vs Green Water: Two Problems, Two Fixes

Water Quality~9 min readUpdated Oct 19, 2026

Both turn your pond green, but string algae and green water are opposite problems. String algae is attached growth you can grab; green water is free-floating algae you can’t. Mixing up their fixes — UV on string algae, or skimming green water — is why so many pond owners treat the wrong thing and get nowhere.

Spot the difference in 10 seconds

Quick ruleIf you can pull it out in strands, it’s string algae. If your hand comes up clear but the water looks like pea soup, it’s green water.

What string algae is — and its surprising upside

String algae (often Spirogyra or related filamentous algae) attaches to surfaces and grows in long strands. It thrives on nutrients in the water plus sunlight on pond surfaces. In small amounts it’s actually beneficial — it consumes nutrients, produces oxygen, and gives koi something to graze. The problem is only when it takes over.

How to fix string algae:

  • Pull it out by hand — the most effective immediate control. Twirl it on a rake or brush, then remove it from the pond (left to rot, it re-releases nutrients).
  • Reduce nutrient load — less feeding, more frequent filter cleaning (feeding, filter care).
  • Add shading — string algae needs light on surfaces; more shade means less growth (shade guide).
  • Don’t rely on UV for string algae — the strands aren’t carried through the UV chamber, so it won’t solve this problem.

What green water is — and why UV is its match

Green water is a bloom of microscopic single-celled algae (phytoplankton) suspended in the water column. It’s the classic “pea soup” — and ironically, it’s often a sign your pond is too efficient at removing the things that would outcompete it. It loves high nutrients and lots of direct sun.

How to fix green water:

  • UV clarifier is the standard fix — the water passes through a UV chamber and the suspended algae cells are killed, then settle or get filtered out. Size the UV to your pond volume and flow rate (filtration guide).
  • Finer mechanical filtration helps remove the dead cells after UV treatment.
  • Cut nutrients at the source — green water won’t return if the nutrient supply drops (feeding less, cleaning filters, water changes).
  • Plant competition — healthy pond plants compete for the same nutrients (plants guide).

Why the fixes get crossed

The classic mix-up: someone buys a UV lamp for string algae, runs it for weeks, and the strands keep growing — then concludes UV doesn’t work. Or someone pulls out handfuls of string algae while the water itself stays green because the real problem was suspended algae all along. Correctly identifying which algae you have is 80% of the fix.

A shared root cause: nutrients and light

Both problems share the same fuel: excess nutrients (especially phosphate and nitrate) plus sunlight. So the long-term strategy is identical even though the short-term fixes differ:

  1. Feed less — the #1 nutrient source in most ponds.
  2. Maintain the filter — clean mechanical media so waste leaves, not rots in place.
  3. Balance plants and shade — plants consume nutrients, shade blocks light.
  4. Control stocking — fewer fish, less waste (stocking density).
  5. Change water regularly — dilute the nutrient soup (water change guide).

Get the diagnosis right, apply the matching fix, and then do the shared maintenance that keeps both away. Algae isn’t your enemy in small amounts — it’s a signal that your pond has excess nutrients and light. Fix the balance and the green goes with it.
