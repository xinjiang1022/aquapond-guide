The Nitrogen Cycle Explained for New Ponds and Aquariums

Water Quality~9 min readUpdated Sep 12, 2026

If you understand only one concept in fish-keeping, make it this one. The nitrogen cycle is the invisible life-support system of every pond and aquarium. Get it running and your fish thrive; skip it and your fish die — usually within the first month.

What the nitrogen cycle actually is

Fish constantly produce waste, mostly as ammonia released through their gills and urine. Ammonia is highly toxic: even 0.5 ppm can stress fish, and 1–2 ppm can be lethal.

Left alone, ammonia would just keep rising. But two families of naturally occurring bacteria save the day:

StepWaste productWho does the workToxicity
1Fish waste → ammonia—Highly toxic
2Ammonia → nitriteNitrosomonas-type bacteriaToxic
3Nitrite → nitrateNitrobacter/Nitrospira-type bacteriaLow toxicity
4Nitrate removedWater changes + plants—

The bacteria live on every surface in the pond — especially inside your filter media, where they form a slimy brown biofilm. That biofilm is your biological filtration.

Why new ponds kill fish: new tank syndrome

A brand-new pond has zero filter bacteria. The moment you add fish, ammonia starts rising, and until a bacteria colony grows large enough to keep up, the water becomes progressively more toxic. Since the bacteria multiply in proportion to the waste available, a sudden ammonia spike often happens right when you add more fish — that is "new tank syndrome."

WarningThe classic timeline: pond built, fish added on day 2, fish gasping by day 7–10, ammonia test reads 1.0+ ppm, first death by week 3. Almost all of it is preventable by cycling first.

How to cycle a new pond (two methods)

Method A: Fishless cycling (recommended)

  1. Fill the pond, dechlorinate, and run pump, filter and aeration.
  2. Add a small ammonia source — a pinch of fish food daily, or pure liquid ammonia dosed to ~2–3 ppm.
  3. Test ammonia and nitrite every 2–3 days.
  4. When ammonia drops to 0 within 24 hours of dosing, the first bacteria colony is established.
  5. Keep dosing until both ammonia and nitrite read 0 within 24 hours — usually 4–8 weeks total.
  6. Do a large water change to remove accumulated nitrate, then add your first fish.

Method B: Fish-in cycling (only for hardy fish, with daily testing)

  1. Add only 1–2 small, hardy fish to the new pond.
  2. Feed tiny amounts — less than half of normal.
  3. Test ammonia and nitrite every day. If either exceeds 0.5 ppm, do a 20–30% water change.
  4. Use a commercial beneficial-bacteria product to speed things up.
  5. Only add more fish once ammonia and nitrite have read 0 for a full week.
TipBottled "instant cycle" bacteria can genuinely help, but they are not magic. They seed the colony faster; they do not replace the weeks of growth the colony needs. Always verify with tests.

What can crash a healthy cycle

  • Over-cleaning the filter: rinsing media in tap water (chlorine kills bacteria) or replacing all media at once removes the colony. Rinse filter media in pond water only, and never clean more than half at a time.
  • Medication: many fish medications, especially antibiotics, are hard on filter bacteria. Run extra aeration and test daily during treatment.
  • Temperature crashes: bacteria slow down in cold water. This is one reason winter is risky for overstocked ponds.
  • Chlorine/chloramine: any tap water added without dechlorinator can wipe out the colony.
  • Sudden overfeeding or mass fish death: a waste spike can outpace even an established colony.

Signs your cycle is struggling

  • Ammonia or nitrite readings that climb after every feeding.
  • Fish sitting at the surface or gasping (early ammonia/nitrite stress).
  • Cloudy or milky water right after a filter clean.
  • That distinct "old fish tank" smell — usually ammonia or a bacterial bloom.

If any of these appear: stop feeding for 2–3 days, do small daily water changes, add aeration, and test until numbers settle. The colony will recover faster than you think — as long as you stop adding waste.
