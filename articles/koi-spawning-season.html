Koi Spawning Season: What to Expect in Your Pond

Fish Health~8 min readUpdated Nov 6, 2026

One warm spring night the pond suddenly sounds like a fountain — fish chasing each other hard through the plants, splashing at the surface. It looks frantic. In almost every case it is not a fight: it is spawning, and the pond is behaving exactly as it should.

What spawning looks like

In spring and early summer, as water temperatures rise into the comfortable range, mature koi — usually over three or four years old — begin chasing. A male drives a female toward the plants or pond edges, nudging her side, until she releases eggs and he milks over them. The splashing and "fighting" appearance is the pairing, not aggression. Expect it to last a day or two and then settle.

TipPlants around the edges are the koi's chosen spawning brush — it is one reason well-planted ponds look naturally busy this season. See plant zones if you plan it deliberately.

Eggs and fry

The sticky eggs attach to plant leaves and any rough surface. In suitable warm water they hatch within days (a few days to around a week, as a general reference — exact timing depends on temperature). The tiny fry then hang on the plants for a few more days before they become free-swimming. This is the part that looks magical if you have ever wanted to raise your own koi.

What happens to the fry

The honest answer: in an open pond, almost none of them survive. Adult koi eat their own eggs and fry without hesitation, and anything a koi misses is usually gone to other predators. A few hardy survivors may appear around the edges weeks later — small, dark, and uncolored. Selective breeding for the show quality you see in shops is a whole project in itself; a typical garden pond is not set up for it.

Should you intervene?

If you just want a calm pond, leave it alone — spawning is harmless and the fish settle within a couple of days. If you actually want to raise fry, you need to move eggs or very young fry to a separate safe tank quickly, with clean water and suitable tiny food. That is a separate project; see quarantine and separate-tank basics before planning it. If a female looks swollen, stretched or uncomfortable beyond a normal spawning season, check it against common koi health issues rather than assuming it is breeding condition.
