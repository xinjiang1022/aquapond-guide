15 Common Koi Diseases: Symptoms and First Steps

Fish Health~14 min readUpdated Sep 12, 2026

Most koi diseases follow the same pattern: stress weakens the fish, a pathogen moves in, and the keeper notices only when symptoms appear. This guide helps you recognise the fifteen most common problems and — just as important — tells you what not to do in a panic.

Before anything elseTest your water. Ammonia, nitrite or a pH crash causes many "disease" symptoms, and treating poor water with medication makes things worse. If water tests are abnormal, fix the water first and reassess after 24 hours.

How to spot a sick koi (the basics)

  • Isolating itself or sitting on the bottom
  • Gasping at the surface or "flashing" (rubbing against walls and liner)
  • Clamped fins, loss of appetite, lethargy
  • Visible spots, cottony patches, red streaks, ulcers or ragged fins
  • Rapid breathing or erratic swimming

Parasites (the most common cause of flashing)

ProblemSigns to look forFirst 24 hours
Ich (white spot)Tiny white grains like salt on fins and body; flashing; rapid breathingRaise temperature slowly if possible; use a dedicated ich treatment; improve aeration
CostiaMilky film on skin, flashing, lethargy; very common after cold snapsSalt bath (0.3–0.5%) is a classic first aid; move fish to warm, clean water
Flukes (gill/skin)Flashing, clamped fins, gasping; gills pale or swollenPraziquantel-based treatment; scrape/scope by a professional to confirm
TrichodinaGrey-white film, flashing, rapid gill movementSame salt-bath approach; improve water quality

Bacterial infections

ProblemSigns to look forFirst 24 hours
UlcersRed, open sores, often with a white edge; usually on body or flanksQuarantine the fish; keep water pristine; consult a vet — antibiotics are usually needed
Fin rotFins look frayed, milky or bloody at the edgesImprove water quality first; treat secondary bacterial infection if it spreads
Red spot / septicemiaRed streaks and blotches under the skin, lethargy, loss of appetiteEmergency: test water, large water change, isolate; seek veterinary advice quickly
Mouth rotWhite or fuzzy growth around the mouth; trouble eatingIsolate, improve water, gentle treatment of the affected area

Fungal and other problems

ProblemSigns to look forFirst 24 hours
Saprolegnia (cotton wool)White/grey cottony tufts, usually over an existing woundIt is a secondary infection — treat the wound and fix the water; antifungal treatment
KHV (Koi Herpesvirus)Gasping, sunken eyes, white patches, gill necrosis; kills fast at 20–25 °CIsolate immediately and contact a vet — KHV is reportable in many regions
Carp poxWaxy, candle-wax-like blisters on the skin; usually harmlessUsually nothing needed; improves with warmer water — don’t burst the blisters
Anchor wormThread-like worms hanging from the skin, red irritation around themRemove visible worms carefully; treat with an antiparasitic for crustaceans
Fish liceSmall flat discs (up to ~5 mm) moving on the body; flashingManual removal + pond-wide antiparasitic treatment
DropsyPinecone-like raised scales, bloated bodyUsually internal (kidney) failure — quarantine, salt bath, consult a vet; often fatal
Swim bladder issuesFloating upside down or struggling to stay levelCheck for constipation/overfeeding; fast the fish, improve temperature stability

The universal first steps (do these for any sick fish)

  1. Test the water — ammonia, nitrite, pH, KH, temperature. Fix anything abnormal before treating anything else.
  2. Quarantine the affected fish in a separate, aerated tank so you can treat precisely and protect the rest of the pond.
  3. Reduce stress — stop feeding, dim bright light, keep temperature stable, add gentle aeration.
  4. Get a diagnosis — for anything beyond obvious parasites, a scrape and microscope check by a pond specialist or aquatic vet is worth every cent. Wrong medication wastes days and harms fish.
  5. Treat the pond, not just the fish — most parasites and bacteria are already in every pond; the goal is to make fish strong enough to resist and remove the trigger (usually water quality or temperature stress).
RememberHealthy, unstressed koi shrug off most pathogens. Overcrowded, overfed ponds in poor water are the ones that get sick. Prevention — water quality, quarantine and not overstocking — is 90% of koi health. See How Many Koi Can Your Pond Hold?
