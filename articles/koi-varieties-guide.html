Koi Varieties Explained: A Beginner’s Guide to Gosanke and Beyond

Koi Basics~9 min readUpdated Sep 21, 2026

Walk into any koi dealer and the Japanese names fly fast — Kohaku, Sanke, Showa, Utsurimono. It sounds complicated until you learn the simple pattern: almost every koi name describes a base colour plus a pattern colour. This guide decodes the main varieties so you can browse a pond with confidence.

Start with the “Big Three”: Gosanke

Gosanke (“go-san-ke”) means the “three great families” — the three most popular and historically valued koi varieties. If you only learn three names, learn these:

VarietyPattern in plain wordsBase colour
KohakuWhite body with red markingsWhite (shiroji)
Sanke (Taisho Sanshoku)White body with red and black markingsWhite
Showa (Showa Sanshoku)Black body with red and white markingsBlack
The easy way to tell Sanke from ShowaSanke is a white fish with red and black accents; black usually appears as small spots on the upper body. Showa is a black fish wrapped in red and white, often with bold black wrapping up over the head and down the sides. If black dominates, it is Showa.

Kohaku: the classic

Kohaku is the original show koi and the best-selling variety worldwide. Judges look for snow-white skin, deep and even red (called hi), and crisp edges where red meets white with no bleeding. A Kohaku with red on the head but none on the eyes or mouth is especially prized.

Sanke: white, red and black

Sanke adds small black markings (sumi) to a Kohaku pattern. Good Sanke balance all three colours across the body; the black spots usually appear on the back and upper flanks rather than the head.

Showa: bold black

Showa has a black base with red and white overlay. Modern “kindai” (modern) Showa show large areas of white, which can confuse beginners — but the black wrapping over the head gives them away.

Other popular varieties

VarietyWhat it looks like
ChagoiSolid tea/brown/green colour; famously the friendliest, most docile koi and quick to hand-feed
OgonSingle-coloured metallic koi (gold, platinum, orange); shiny, reflective skin
UtsurimonoBlack base with white, red or yellow bands (Shiro Utsuri, Hi Utsuri, Ki Utsuri)
BekkoWhite, red or yellow body with small black tortoiseshell spots
AsagiBlue-grey net-like pattern on the back with red cheeks and belly — one of the oldest varieties
ShusuiThe German (scaleless or partially scaled) version of Asagi, with a row of large mirror scales along the back
Goshiki“Five colours” — an Asagi-style base overlaid with Kohaku red markings
TanchoAny variety with a single round red patch on the head, like the Japanese flag; Kohaku Tancho is most famous
DoitsuNot a colour variety — it means “German scale” type: scaleless or with a few large mirror scales, in any colour pattern
Tip for beginnersOgon and Chagoi are excellent first koi. They are hardy, grow fast and are easy to appreciate without knowing show judging rules. Fancy Gosanke can be expensive and pattern judgement takes years to develop.

Understanding koi colour vocabulary

A handful of words appear in almost every variety name:

  • Hi — red markings
  • Shiro — white
  • Sumi — black markings
  • Ki — yellow
  • Aka — solid/deep red (e.g. aka muji, an all-red koi)
  • Ginrin — sparkling diamond scales added to any variety
  • Doitsu — German scale type

Combine them and names start making sense: Yamabuki Ogon is a golden-yellow metallic koi; Ginrin Kohaku is a Kohaku with sparkling scales.

What makes a good koi, regardless of variety

Show judging is nuanced, but the basics are the same for every variety:

  1. Healthy skin and conformation — glossy skin, deep body, no deformities (see our buyer’s checklist).
  2. Clean colour edges — sharp boundaries between colours; no blurring or freckling.
  3. Balanced pattern — markings distributed pleasingly along the body, not all piled at one end.
  4. Pattern stability — colours that will hold as the fish grows. Juvenile patterns can change a lot, so buy from a dealer who can advise honestly.

Remember that price mostly reflects show quality and lineage, not how much you will enjoy the fish. A healthy $30 Chagoi that eats from your hand often gives more daily pleasure than a high-end show koi that hides all day.
