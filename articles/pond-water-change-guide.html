How to Do a Pond Water Change the Right Way

Water Quality~9 min readUpdated Oct 19, 2026

Water changes are the cheapest water-quality tool you own — but done wrong, they shock koi and set back your filter bacteria. The secret is boring and reliable: small, frequent, temperature-matched changes instead of dramatic ones. Here’s the step-by-step.

Why change water at all?

Filters process ammonia and nitrite, but they can’t remove everything. Dissolved organics, nitrates, phosphate and invisible contaminants build up over time — and the only way they leave the pond is by being diluted out. A regular water change:

  • Dilutes nitrates and phosphates — the fuel for algae blooms (algae guide).
  • Replaces trace minerals that koi need for health.
  • Reduces dissolved organic load on your filter and skimmer.
  • Improves clarity — the most visible quick win for murky water (murky water guide).

How much and how often

There’s no universal formula — it depends on stocking, feeding and filtration. These are common starting points, not rules:

Pond situationTypical changeFrequency
Well-filtered, lightly stocked~10% of volumeWeekly or bi-weekly
Average stocked koi pond~10–15% of volumeWeekly
Heavily stocked / heavy feeding~15–20% of volumeWeekly, test water in between
Problem water (high nitrates, algae)Small 10% changesMore frequently, not larger
The golden ruleChange small and often, not big and rarely. A 50% sudden change is a temperature and chemistry shock to every fish and to your filter bacteria — it can do more harm than the build-up it removes.

The step-by-step routine

  1. Prepare the water first. If your tap water is chlorinated, treat it with a dechlorinator, or fill a holding tank a day ahead. Never add untreated tap water straight to the pond.
  2. Match temperature. Koi tolerate gradual change; a few degrees is fine, but avoid large swings. In hot weather, adding cooler water slowly matters.
  3. Remove water from the bottom, not the surface. Waste settles low — a bottom drain or pump pulls out the dirtiest water.
  4. Vacuum or net the bottom during the removal to lift settled sludge (this is where most of the “dirt” lives).
  5. Replace slowly. Trickle the new water back in rather than dumping — a hose on low or a small pump works well.
  6. Restart circulation. Keep the pump running during the change so new water mixes evenly and fish never sit in dead water.

Watch the filter — and the clock

  • Never clean your bio-filter during a water change. Change water one day, clean filter media another. Doing both at once removes too much bacteria and can spike ammonia (filter maintenance).
  • Avoid water changes during cold snaps or heat waves — fish are already stressed by temperature; don’t add chemistry stress on top.
  • Time it for the warmest, calmest part of the day in spring and fall.
  • Test before and after — pH, ammonia and nitrite readings tell you if the change helped or over-reached (parameters guide).

When NOT to change water

SituationWhy hold off
Winter, water below ~8°C (46°F)Fish and bacteria are dormant; changes add stress with no filter benefit
During a disease treatmentChanging water can dilute medications — follow the treatment protocol instead
Freshly cycled pondLet the new filter establish; big changes slow the cycle (nitrogen cycle)
Tap water is contaminatedTreat or source water first; bad water in is worse than old water out

Your water-change cheat sheet

StepDoDon’t
Amount10–15% typical, small and frequentHuge sudden changes
Water prepDechlorinate, temperature-matchRaw tap water straight in
RemovalFrom the bottom, with sludge vacuumOnly skimming the surface
ReplacementSlow trickle, pump runningFast dump
FilterLeave it alone on change dayClean media at the same time

Master the boring water change and half your pond problems — algae, murk, declining water quality — quietly lose their fuel. It’s the least glamorous maintenance you’ll do, and the most consistently effective.
