pH Crash and Drift in Koi Ponds: Causes and Safe Fixes

Water Quality~10 min readUpdated Sep 28, 2026

A slowly drifting pH is normal; a sudden crash is an emergency. Koi handle a wide pH range comfortably — what hurts them is rapid change. This guide explains why pH swings happen, the role of KH (carbonate hardness), and how to stabilise pH without dangerous chemicals.

The rule that matters: stability beats the number

Healthy koi ponds routinely run anywhere from roughly pH 6.5 to 8.5, and koi can live well in most of that range. What kills is the rate of change. A pH that drops from 8.0 to 6.5 over a few hours stresses every fish in the pond — skin, gills and blood chemistry are all affected. So the goal is never “the perfect pH number”; it is a stable pH number.

Emergency signIf fish are gasping at the surface, flashing wildly, or swimming erratically right after a rain, storm or heavy algae die-off, test pH immediately. A rapid drop is a medical emergency for the whole pond.

Why pH drifts and crashes

pH moves when the balance between acids and buffers shifts. The main drivers:

CauseWhat happens
Low carbonate hardness (KH)KH is the pond’s pH shock absorber. With low KH, daily acid production (from respiration and the nitrogen cycle) quickly drives pH down — the classic recipe for a crash
Heavy rainRain is slightly acidic and dilute; big storms can push pH down fast, especially in low-KH ponds
Algae die-offDead algae rot into acids and CO₂; a sudden green-water crash (UV working overtime) can drag pH down sharply
Overfeeding and overstockingMore waste, more acid produced by bacteria, more buffering capacity consumed
Tap water changesLocal tap water may have different pH and alkalinity than the pond; large changes swing the number

KH is the real hero

KH (carbonate hardness, alkalinity) is the pond’s buffer — it neutralises acids as they form, keeping pH steady. This is why KH matters more than pH itself:

  • Test both pH and KH together; KH is the leading indicator.
  • Most ponds do best with KH above roughly 100–120 ppm (mg/L) — enough buffer for normal daily acid load. Exact needs vary with stocking and feeding, so treat this as a general reference.
  • If KH is low and pH drifts daily, raise KH before chasing pH. Raising KH is also the gentlest way to lift pH — see below.

Safe fixes for drift

  1. Measure first: test pH and KH at the same time of day for a week to see the daily swing. A morning-to-evening swing under ~0.5 is normal; larger swings mean low buffering.
  2. Raise KH with sodium bicarbonate (baking soda): the standard keeper’s tool for boosting alkalinity. Add it gradually — small doses over days, never a single big dose, and test after each dose. The target is stable KH, not a specific number.
  3. Do smaller, more frequent water changes instead of one big one — gentler on both chemistry and fish.
  4. Reduce the acid load: feed less, check stocking, and clear dead plant/algae matter promptly.
  5. Maintain aeration: good oxygen levels help the biological processes that keep water chemistry stable (aeration guide).
Do not use pH-up/pH-down products as a habit. Liquid pH adjusters create exactly the rapid swings you are trying to avoid. Fix the buffering (KH) and the pH steadies itself.

Handling a crash in progress

If pH has already dropped and fish are distressed:

  • Slow, partial water change (10–15%) with well-aerated water of known quality — this both dilutes acids and adds some buffering.
  • Raise KH gradually (baking soda, small repeated doses) — raising KH lifts pH gently and holds it there.
  • Boost aeration hard. Oxygen helps fish survive the stress and supports bacteria.
  • Hold feeding until stable. Every pellet adds acid load while the system recovers.
  • Never try to jump pH fast. A quick chemical fix from 6.0 to 8.0 is as dangerous as the crash itself.

Prevention checklist

DoneHabit
☐Test pH and KH weekly (more in summer and after storms)
☐Keep KH above roughly 100–120 ppm with gradual baking-soda dosing if needed
☐Feed to the fish’s appetite in 5 minutes — no leftovers
☐Clear dead algae, leaves and plants promptly
☐Do regular small water changes, not occasional huge ones
☐Keep aeration running year-round

Stable pH is less about chemistry magic and more about good, boring management: buffer, feed, aerate, test. Get those four right and pH takes care of itself.
