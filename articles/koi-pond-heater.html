Koi Pond Heater: Do You Need One in Winter?

Winter Care~9 min readUpdated Nov 4, 2026

The instinct is to warm the pond the moment frost arrives. But koi are cold-water fish: they slow down, stop feeding and spend the winter at the bottom of a deep, partly-frozen pool. Heating helps only in specific situations — and a badly chosen heater can do more harm than good.

Do koi actually need a heater?

In most outdoor ponds, no. Koi overwinter naturally as long as a layer of water remains liquid under the ice — a hole for gas exchange is all they need. Our winter pond care guide covers the basics: keep the pump running gently, stop feeding, and leave the fish undisturbed.

Heating starts to make sense when your pond could freeze solid, or when you deliberately keep koi active. The problem is not the cold itself; it is water turning to ice all the way through.

When a heater is genuinely useful

  • Very small or shallow ponds.A shallow container can freeze to the bottom, leaving fish with nowhere to go deeper. See Pond Depth in Winter.
  • Situations where a total freeze is likely.Cold climates with weeks of deep frost can defeat an unheated small pond.
  • Recovery or quarantine use.Raising temperature can help a recovering koi eat again, as part of a vet-guided plan — see Quarantine.
  • Indoor or greenhouse tubs kept over winter with fish you want to grow on.
WarningDo not heat a large outdoor pond you never needed to heat before. A warm pond keeps koi eating when their metabolism is slowing, keeps plants active under ice, and the heater itself costs a lot to run through the winter.

The heater options, compared

OptionWhat it doesBest for
Pond de-icer (floating heater)Keeps a small ice hole open, not the whole pond warmOpen ponds that would otherwise seal over
Aquarium-style immersion heaterHolds a set temperatureSmall indoor tubs / quarantine tanks
Air pump to break surface iceCirculates water and keeps an ice hole without heatingMost small outdoor ponds in mild winters
TipA floating pond de-icer is the middle ground: it keeps a breathing hole open without trying to heat the whole pond — usually what outdoor koi actually need.

Sizing and safety

For immersion heaters in small tubs, manufacturers publish wattage guidance per volume — treat those numbers as general reference for your water volume (see How to Measure Pond Volume). In short:

  • Use a heater rated for roughly your volume, not a stronger one.
  • Install it with a thermostat so it holds a target temperature rather than running flat out.
  • Keep the heater fully submerged and away from the liner, and check the cord for wear each winter.
  • Never mix a heater with a recently drained pond — dry elements fail fast.

Heating vs covering vs aerating

A common alternative to a heater is simply covering the pond for the coldest weeks — a tarp over the frame, weighted down, slows heat loss and keeps snow off. In many climates that plus an air pump is enough to survive the winter, and it costs nothing to run. Reserve actual heating for when a cover or air pump is not enough, or for the recovery-tank cases mentioned above. Budget for the electricity before buying: heaters run for weeks at a time and the bill adds up quickly.

If your pond is deep and overwintered safely last year, you probably do not need to change anything this winter. Start with an air pump for a breathing hole, and add a heater only if your pond actually froze solid last time.
