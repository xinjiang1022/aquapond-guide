Koi Scale Loss and Injury: Scratches, Tears and How to Help Healing

Fish Health~6 min readUpdated Nov 27, 2026

Missing scales, pale scrapes or a ragged fin can look alarming, but koi recover well from many minor injuries when the water stays clean and stress is kept low. The first job is to work out how the damage happened, separate a simple wound from a sign of illness, and avoid rushing into treatments that may do more harm than good. This guide walks through the common injury types, a calm inspection routine, and the conditions that let skin and scales grow back.

The short answer

Most scale loss and scrapes come from a physical event: hitting the liner or equipment during a fright, rough handling when netting, a predator attack, chasing among breeding fish, or a fin snagged on a sharp edge. A clean, shallow scrape with no redness, swelling or open ulcer usually settles in clean water over time. Wounds that grow, look red or fuzzy, expose raw skin, or sit alongside flashing, clamped fins and poor appetite need closer attention and possibly specialist advice.

RememberClean water is the foundation of healing. Medication cannot compensate for ammonia, nitrite or poor filtration, and it often adds stress to an already injured fish. Test the water before treating the wound.

Common types of koi injury

Injuries differ in how they look and what they mean. Match what you see to the pattern below, then trace the likely source in the pond.

A calm inspection routine

1. Watch the fish before netting it

Stand quietly and observe how the fish swims, feeds and interacts. An injured fish that still moves normally and takes food is usually in better shape than one that hangs back, droops its fins or refuses food. Repeated rubbing or flashing suggests irritation that may be parasitic rather than a plain wound; see the guide to koi flashing and rubbing. Appetite changes are covered in why koi stop eating.

2. Check the water first

Test ammonia, nitrite, pH and temperature, and confirm the filter, pump and aeration are running. Polluted water delays healing and lets small wounds develop into open problems. Parameters are explained in the water parameters guide, and a steady testing habit in the pond testing routine. If readings are off, correct the water before adding any wound treatment.

3. Look closely at the wound

If you need to net the fish, keep handling to a minimum and keep it wet. Note the location, size and whether skin is broken. A smooth, dry-looking scrape is different from an open, red or oozing ulcer. Watch for swelling, a pale or fuzzy coating, bleeding, and whether the surrounding skin has lost its normal sheen. Main health conditions are listed in common koi diseases so you can compare symptoms rather than guess.

4. Trace the source in the pond

Look for what the fish could have hit: sharp corners on the liner, exposed pipework, rough rock edges, a tight skimmer opening, or equipment the fish can wedge behind. Predator pressure is a frequent cause of back and tail wounds; deterrents are covered in the guide to protecting koi from predators. Frightened fish that dash into walls are also covered in why koi jump and bolt. Overcrowding raises stress and chasing; see pond stocking density.

5. Reduce stress while it heals

Keep feeding regular and calm, avoid netting or disturbing the pond unnecessarily, and make sure the injured fish can get away from more assertive tankmates. New arrivals that carry stress or parasites are a common trigger; the quarantine guide for new koi explains why isolation matters before adding fish to the main pond.

Healing and scale regrowth

Healthy koi regrow scales over time, and fin tissue often closes a small tear gradually. The timeline varies with water temperature, the size of the wound and the fish's overall condition, so treat any specific number as a rough guide rather than a guarantee. New scales may sit a little paler or smaller at first and blend in later. The clearest signs of recovery are steady feeding, normal swimming, and a wound that stays clean rather than spreading or swelling.

When to seek specialist advice

Get advice from a reputable aquatic specialist or veterinarian if the wound deepens or enlarges, shows redness, swelling, pus or a fuzzy coating, exposes raw flesh, or the fish stops feeding, becomes listless, develops clamped fins or keeps rubbing. Deep predator wounds and open ulcers are higher risk than a surface scrape. Do not rely on online observation alone when a wound is worsening.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Treating the wound before testing water: Polluted water keeps the injury open; medication alone will not fix that.
  • Netting and handling repeatedly: Extra stress and rough contact can lift more scales and slow recovery.
  • Assuming every scrape is a predator: Collisions, handling and breeding chase cause most surface abrasions.
  • Ignoring illness-linked signs: Redness, ulcers, flashing and poor appetite together point to a health problem, not just an accident.
