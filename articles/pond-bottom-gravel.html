Should You Put Rocks and Gravel on the Pond Bottom?

Pond Design~9 min readUpdated Nov 5, 2026

One of the first debates in koi keeping is also one of the most visible: leave the liner floor bare, or cover it with rocks and gravel? Both sides are defending a real trade-off — and the honest answer depends on how much cleaning you are prepared to do.

The case for a bare bottom

Bare-bottom ponds are the default in serious koi keeping, and for good reason:

  • Easy to clean.A pond vacuum or hose wand glides across the liner; every leaf, pellet and fish waste pellet is visible and removable.
  • You can see problems.Uneaten food after feeding, new waste patterns, a sick koi hiding on the floor — all obvious at a glance.
  • No anaerobic pockets.Muck cannot settle into hidden gaps, which keeps ammonia spikes (see the nitrogen cycle) easier to manage.

The downside is purely visual: a bare bottom looks plain, especially in a garden pond that is meant to look natural.

The case for rocks and gravel

Gravel and river stones look the part, and they do real work: the stones host a biofilm of beneficial bacteria that supplements the filter, and they protect the liner from UV damage and from koi nosing along it. For wildlife ponds and mixed garden ponds, a stony bottom is the norm.

The hidden cost of gravel

The problem is not the stones themselves — it is what settles between them. Over months, uneaten food and fish waste pack into the gaps. When that layer goes anaerobic, it releases gases and quietly loads ammonia and hydrogen sulphide into the water. This is exactly the muck covered in our bottom sludge guide. By the time you notice cloudy water or a smell, the sludge has been building for a while.

WarningA gravel bottom is not "free filtration". If you cannot vacuum it properly every season, it will store waste for you — and hand it back all at once during a warm spell.

The compromise most keepers land on

Bare bottomFull gravelMixed (compromise)
Visual lookPlainNaturalNatural, partially
Cleaning effortLowHighMedium
Waste hidingNoneA lotContained

The middle path: leave the feeding area and deepest zone bare so you can clean it, and ring the edges with larger decorative stones that are easy to lift and hose. Avoid small pea-gravel spread across the whole floor — it traps the most muck for the least reward. Whichever you choose, the filter still has to do the heavy lifting; read how pond filtration works and see the liner guide before you start moving stones around.
