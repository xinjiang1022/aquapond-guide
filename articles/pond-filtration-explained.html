Pond Filtration Explained: Mechanical, Biological and UV

Equipment~11 min readUpdated Sep 12, 2026

Filter shopping is confusing because every box promises the world. Strip away the marketing and a pond filter does only three jobs: trap debris, house beneficial bacteria, and (optionally) kill algae. Here is how each one works, what matters, and how to size it.

The three jobs of a pond filter

StageWhat it removesHow it worksEssential?
MechanicalLeaves, fish waste, uneaten food, muckFoam, brushes, netting or a settlement chamber physically trap solidsYes
BiologicalAmmonia and nitrite (toxic)Bacteria living on porous media convert toxins into nitrateYes — the most important

Mechanical filtration: the obvious part

Mechanical media physically strain the water. Pumped water flows through foam pads, brushes or a settlement chamber, leaving the debris behind for you to rinse away. It keeps the water clear of particles — but it does nothing for the invisible toxins that actually hurt fish.

Key points:

  • A settlement chamber or vortex before the pump lets heavy waste drop out of suspension — it is the single most efficient mechanical stage for koi ponds.
  • Mechanical media needs cleaning regularly — once a week to once a month depending on the pond. Rinse it in pond water, never tap water.

Biological filtration: the part that keeps fish alive

Biological filtration is not a gadget — it is a surface area. Beneficial bacteria (the same ones from the nitrogen cycle) need somewhere to live, and the more surface area your media provides, the bigger the colony and the more waste it can process.

The best biological media is lightweight, porous and permanent:

  • Bio-balls / plastic media — huge surface area, easy to clean, never wears out. A top choice for koi ponds.
  • Lava rock / ceramic rings — good porosity, but heavy and harder to clean.
  • Japanese matting (bakki-style) — excellent for shower/bakki filters, huge surface area with excellent oxygenation.
TipFor a koi pond, plan for 1–2% of pond volume as dedicated biological media. A 2,000-gallon pond needs roughly 20–40 gallons of bio-media to comfortably support a typical stocking level.

UV clarifiers: the green-water cure

Green, pea-soup water is not a bacteria problem — it is millions of single-celled algae. A UV clarifier runs water past a UV bulb; the light damages the algae cells, they clump together and are then caught by the mechanical filter. It does not kill string algae on the walls, and it does not improve water chemistry.

  • Size the UV for your pond: roughly 10 watts per 1,000 gallons is a common baseline, more if your pond gets heavy sun.
  • Replace the bulb every 12 months — even if it still looks fine, UV output fades.
  • Keep the quartz sleeve clean; a dirty sleeve blocks most of the UV.

Sizing and maintenance rules of thumb

  • Filter rating: buy a filter rated for 1.5–2× your actual pond volume — ratings assume light stocking.
  • Flow: the whole pond volume should turn over at least once per hour. See How to Choose the Right Pond Pump.
  • Never clean everything: clean one stage or half the media at a time, so the bacteria colony survives. Rinse in pond water.
  • Never replace all media: bio-media is a living ecosystem; "old" media is the best media.
  • Seasonal check: in spring, restart filtration gradually and test ammonia/nitrite daily for the first week.
Reality checkNo filter can fix an overstocked, overfed pond. Filtration processes waste — it does not create capacity. If your water is permanently cloudy or ammonia never reads zero, the filter is not the problem; the stocking or feeding is. See stocking density.
