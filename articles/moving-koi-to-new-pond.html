How to Move Koi Safely to a New Pond: A Step-by-Step Relocation Guide

Koi Care~6 min readUpdated Nov 22, 2026

Moving koi to a new pond — relocating the whole setup, upgrading to a larger pond, or transferring fish between two existing ponds — comes down to preparation, gentle handling and not rushing the release. Stress, low oxygen in the container and mismatched water conditions are the common ways a move goes wrong. This guide walks through the sequence in order, with general reference timelines rather than fixed rules.

Get the new pond ready first

The most important rule: move the fish into a pond that is already ready, not the other way around. A new or rebuilt pond needs its filter running and its water cycled before koi go in; an uncycled pond cannot handle fish waste, and ammonia spikes after a move are a real risk. Confirm the following before you catch a single fish:

  • The new pond holds dechlorinated water, with pump, filter and aeration running for several days.
  • Ammonia and nitrite read at zero, or the pond has cycled long enough to process waste safely.
  • Temperature and pH are broadly close to the old pond; large differences cause shock at release.

If you are unsure whether the new pond has cycled, read the nitrogen cycle guide first. Waiting a few extra days is easier than rescuing stressed fish from an immature pond.

Choose the right time to move

Relocation stress is easiest to manage in mild, stable weather. Avoid moving koi in extreme heat, hard frost, heavy rain or a cold snap, when the fish are already under seasonal stress. Mid-morning on a calm, overcast day is generally a gentle window.

ConditionHow it affects the moveApproach
Mild, overcast, stable temperatureLow stress, comfortable handlingGood window to proceed
Hot, sunny weatherWarm water loses oxygen fast; fish overheat in containersMove early morning, keep containers shaded and cool

Active spawning seasonDisturbing fish mid-spawn adds stress to adults and eggsWait until spawning settles, if possible
TipStop feeding the fish for a day or so before the move. A lighter gut means less waste in the transport water; this is a general practice, not a strict fasting rule, and adjust it for very small or delicate fish.

What you need to catch and transport them

Assemble your equipment before lowering the net into the old pond, so the fish spends as little time out of settled water as possible:

  • Soft mesh nets with fine, flexible material — avoid rough knotty nets that snag fins and scales.
  • Containers or bags large enough for the fish to lie comfortably, with enough water to cover its body.
  • Oxygen or aeration for any trip beyond a short while — a bubbler in a tub, or an oxygen-filled bag.
  • Shade and insulation — keep the container out of direct sun and away from drafts.

Catching the fish gently

Panicked netting causes more injury than the move itself. Herd the fish slowly toward a corner or shallow edge rather than chasing it around the pond. Bring the net up under the fish and lift in one smooth motion; never drag a koi through the air by its tail or squeeze the body. Support the fish under the body as you transfer it to the waiting container. Work one fish at a time if you can — cramming several into one small tub raises waste, lowers oxygen and increases fin damage.

Transport: keep water calm and oxygenated

During the journey, the priorities are stable temperature, enough oxygen and minimal sloshing. Keep the container shaded, secure it so it cannot tip, and avoid long stops in a hot car or direct sun. The container water warms, cools or accumulates waste over time, so shorter trips are always easier.

WarningDo not pour old pond water into the new pond. Transport water can carry waste, parasites or disease; release the fish, but leave the container water behind. This matches the basic hygiene in the acclimation guide.

Releasing into the new pond: match the water first

Even a prepared new pond can differ in temperature, pH and hardness from the old one. The release step lets the fish adjust rather than dropping it straight in:

  • Float the sealed bag or rest the container in the new pond so the temperature gradually equalises.
  • Over several minutes, add small amounts of new-pond water to the container in stages.
  • Once the water has blended, tip the fish gently into the pond rather than tossing it in.

If the two ponds are close and share similar water, this matching step can be brief; if conditions differ noticeably, blend slowly. See the quarantine guide for whether any fish should be held back before joining an established group.

After the move: what to watch for

It is normal for relocated koi to hide, stay near the bottom or eat little for the first day or two. Watch for the following instead, which suggest a problem beyond ordinary stress:

  • Gasping at the surface or hanging near the water outlet
  • Listless fish that do not move away when approached
  • Rubbing or flashing, clamped fins or visible skin changes
  • Cloudy, foul-smelling water or poor appetite lasting more than a couple of days

Keep feeding light for the first few days, and test ammonia and nitrite during settling in. A water-change routine that keeps waste under control is covered in the water change guide.
