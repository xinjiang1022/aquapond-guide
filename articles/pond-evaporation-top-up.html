Pond Water Evaporation: Normal Water Loss, Topping Up and Spotting a Leak

Water Care~6 min readUpdated Nov 24, 2026

A falling water level usually causes alarm, but much of the time it is simply evaporation — especially in warm, sunny, windy weather. The skill is working out whether what you are seeing is normal water loss, splashing from the pump or waterfall, or a genuine leak. This guide covers what drives evaporation, how to compare the three causes, how to top up safely without shocking the fish, and when to stop refilling and start investigating the liner.

The short answer

Water leaves a pond by evaporation into the air, by spray and splash from moving features, or through a leak in the liner, joints or plumbing. Evaporation climbs with heat, wind, direct sun and a large open surface, and falls on cool, humid, still days. Splash follows the pump and waterfall; a leak tends to keep showing up even when equipment is switched off. Before you add water to compensate, work out which cause you are dealing with — because refilling a leak masks the problem rather than fixing it.

RememberDo not judge water loss against someone else’s pond. Surface area, depth, climate, shade and features all change the rate; compare your own pond against its normal level on similar days.

What drives evaporation

Several conditions speed up how fast water turns to vapor:

FactorEffect on water loss
Air and water temperatureWarmer days hold more moisture, so evaporation rises in hot weather and drops in cool months
WindBreezes sweep away moist air above the surface and noticeably speed drying
Direct sunlightOpen, sunny ponds warm faster and lose more water than shaded ones
Surface areaA wide, shallow pond exposes more water to the air than a deep, narrow one of the same volume
Water featuresWaterfalls, streams and fountains increase splash and spray in addition to surface evaporation

How much loss is normal?

There is no one safe number that fits every pond, because evaporation depends on the local climate and the factors above. The reliable method is to learn your own baseline: mark the waterline after a calm, settled day, note the weather, and check the level again over the next few days. A slow, steady drop that tracks warm and windy spells is typical evaporation. A drop that does not match the weather, or that continues unchanged through cool nights, deserves closer inspection.

If your pond has plants that shade the surface, they both slow evaporation and reduce algae pressure — see the pond shade guide and floating plants for shade.

Evaporation, splash or leak: how to tell them apart

SignEvaporationSplash or sprayPossible leak
Timing of the dropGreatest on hot, dry, windy days; steadier overnightHappens mainly while the pump or waterfall runsContinues day and night, pump on or off
Surrounding groundBanks and liner edges stay dryWet patches around the waterfall, stream or spray zoneDamp soil or standing water near the liner or pipework
Level with pump offStill drops slowly, matching evaporationLoss slows sharply once flow stopsKeeps falling after the pump is switched off

A simple test settles most doubts: stop the pump, mark the level, and observe over a day or two. If the water keeps dropping with no water moving, a leak is the more likely explanation. Splash and spray from a waterfall are covered alongside flow design in the waterfall guide.

How to top up safely

Once the loss is evaporation, topping up is routine — but the replacement water deserves the same care as a water change:

  • Remove chlorine first: Tap water carries chlorine or chloramine that harms gills and beneficial bacteria. Treat the fresh water with conditioner before it enters the pond; details are in the dechlorinating tap water guide.
  • Add gradually: Large, sudden top-ups can shift temperature and chemistry at once. Smaller additions spread over time are gentler on fish and filter.
  • Match temperature where possible: Very cold tap water into warm pond water can shock fish; let replacement water temper before adding it.
  • Retest after a big refill: Check pH, KH and the general parameters once the water has mixed. Top-ups dilute salts but not the salts already in the water — note that salt does not evaporate, only water does, per the pond salt guide.
  • Watch stocking and waste: Refilling does not remove waste. If nitrate or waste has built up, a proper water change is the fix, not a top-up — see the water change guide.

Keep an eye on pump and skimmer intake

As the level falls, the skimmer and pump intake can draw air or expose the intake. A pump running dry loses prime and can be damaged, and the skimmer stops collecting surface debris. Keep water high enough to cover the intake and let the skimmer mouth sit correctly at the surface. Pump placement and intake rules are covered in choosing the right pond pump, and surface debris collection in the skimmer guide.

When to suspect a real leak

Treat the situation as a leak when the level keeps dropping after the pump is off, when wet ground appears around the liner or pipe joints, or when the loss is far beyond your marked baseline in ordinary weather. Check liner edges, folds, underlay and any pipe penetrations first; the step-by-step hunt and patch are laid out in how to patch a small pond liner leak. If water is vanishing through a concealed structural area, a pond professional should inspect it before it worsens.

Quick level-maintenance checklist

  • Mark the waterline and record the weather for your own evaporation baseline.
  • Compare daytime and overnight levels, and the level with the pump on and off.
  • Rule out waterfall splash and spray before suspecting the liner.
  • Top up slowly with dechlorinated, temperature-matched water.
  • Retest water parameters after any large refill.
  • If the level keeps falling with equipment off, stop refilling and investigate the leak.
