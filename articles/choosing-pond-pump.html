How to Choose the Right Pond Pump (Flow Rate Explained)

Equipment~9 min readUpdated Sep 12, 2026

The most common pond-pump mistake is buying one that is too small — and the second most common is buying one that is too big. Both waste money. This guide shows you how to calculate exactly what your pond needs in three simple numbers.

Step 1: Know your pond volume

Every pump decision starts with volume. Measure your pond in gallons (or litres) as accurately as you can:

Pond shapeFormula
RectangleLength (ft) × Width (ft) × Avg depth (ft) × 7.48 = gallons
CircleDiameter² (ft) × 0.785 × Avg depth (ft) × 7.48 = gallons
Irregular / kidneyEstimate as 80–85% of the bounding rectangle

For a pond with sloping sides, use the average depth, not the deepest point. Better to underestimate your filter needs and overestimate for pumps.

Step 2: Calculate the required flow (turnover)

The golden rule for koi ponds: the pump should move at least the entire pond volume through the filter every hour — that is a "1× turnover per hour." Heavily stocked koi ponds do better at 1.5–2×.

ExampleA 2,500-gallon koi pond with average stocking needs ≈2,500–3,500 GPH (gallons per hour) of actual, at-the-filter flow. Aim for the higher end if you plan to add fish.

Step 3: Adjust for head height and friction (the part everyone forgets)

Pump ratings (e.g. "3,000 GPH") are measured with the pump at water level pushing water straight up zero feet. Real ponds are harder:

  • Head height: every foot the water rises above the pond surface reduces flow. A pump rated at 3,000 GPH may deliver only 1,800 GPH at 6 ft of head.
  • Pipe friction: long hose runs, narrow pipe and elbows all reduce flow. Use the largest practical pipe (1.5–2 inch for koi ponds) and keep runs short.
  • Rule of thumb: read the pump's flow curve and size so that flow at your head height still meets your turnover target.
Common mistakeBuying a "3,000 GPH" pump, plumbing it up a 5-ft waterfall through 30 ft of 1-inch hose, and ending up with ~1,000 GPH of actual flow. Always size off the flow curve at your real head height, not the box number.

Submersible vs external pumps

SubmersibleExternal (dry / centrifugal)
InstallationDrop in the pond, plug inPlumbed outside the pond, below water level
Best forSmall-to-medium ponds, waterfalls, retrofitsMedium-to-large koi ponds, high flow, gravity-fed systems
ProsCheap, quiet, simplePowerful, efficient, easy to service, longer life
ConsAdds heat to pond, harder to clean, shorter lifeCosts more, needs plumbing and priming

For ponds under ~1,500 gallons, a quality submersible is usually the sensible choice. For serious koi ponds, an external pump connected to a gravity-fed filter system is the long-term answer.

Extra features worth paying for

  • Variable speed / controller: lets you run high flow in summer and low in winter — saves electricity and protects fish.
  • Energy efficiency: a pump running 24/7/365 is a big cost; compare watts per gallon moved, not just purchase price.
  • Pre-filter / leaf trap: protects the pump and reduces cleaning.
  • GFCI: your outdoor circuit must be GFCI-protected, whatever pump you choose.

Three-number summary

NumberHow to get it
1. Pond volumeMeasure and calculate in gallons
2. Target flowVolume × 1 to 2 (turnovers per hour)
3. Pump modelPick the pump whose flow at your head height meets the target
