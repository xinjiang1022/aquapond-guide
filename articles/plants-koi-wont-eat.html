Pond Plants That Koi Don't Usually Eat

Pond Plants~7 min readUpdated Nov 7, 2026

A koi pond and a garden pond are not the same project. Koi dig, root around, and taste everything — the soft oxygenating plants that look perfect in a wildlife pond can be gone within a week. If you want plants that actually survive, lean on tougher marginal and upright species rather than fighting the fish.

What koi tend to eat

Plant typeKoi's attitude
Soft oxygenators (hornwort, elodea)Nibbled and torn; rarely lasts
Young, tender shootsTasted first, dug up second
Tender floating plants (duckweed)Eaten — often welcome, actually
Tough, leathery marginalsMostly ignored once established
Upright reeds and grassesLeft alone; they just get big
TipThere is no plant koi will never touch — a hungry, bored or young koi will experiment. The goal is to pick things they mostly leave alone, then protect them while they establish.

The shortlist that usually survives

As a general reference, koi ponds do well with hard-leaved marginal plants and upright species rather than soft submerged ones. Think reeds, rushes, and hardy water-edge plants — they provide shade and cover without becoming a salad. For depth zones and how to arrange them, see plant zones by depth, and the beginner plant guide for the roles floating and oxygenating plants still play even when koi are around.

Protect plants while they establish

A new plant is at its most tempting in its first weeks. Pot it rather than planting it straight into the gravel, and weight the pot down. A simple guard of mesh around the pot — or even a few larger stones on top — keeps koi from rooting around until the plant is toughened up. Once it is established, most koi lose interest.

And remember: a few koi-chewed plants are not a failure. Koi ponds are meant to be slightly plainer than wildlife ponds, and the balance of plants you choose also feeds into algae control — see algae control basics for how much plant coverage actually matters.

A note on water lilies

Many beginner koi owners are disappointed by lilies: koi will nibble the young lily pads and occasionally root around the basket. It is not impossible to grow them, but treat them as a luxury rather than a given — protect the basket, expect some chewing, and choose tough, established plants. The shade they provide is genuinely useful in summer (see why shade matters), which is exactly why people keep trying, but set expectations honestly: a koi pond is a lily pond with extra steps, not the other way around.
