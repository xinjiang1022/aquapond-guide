How Cold Is Too Cold for Koi? Temperature Limits and Ice Safety

Koi Care~6 min readUpdated Nov 21, 2026

Koi are cold-water fish and handle cool weather far better than tropical species, but there is a point where falling temperature, shallow water and surface ice turn from normal winter conditions into real risk. This guide explains the general temperature ranges, what ice means for your fish, and which signs should prompt action. Figures below are general reference ranges, not fixed medical thresholds.

How koi respond to falling temperature

As water cools, a koi’s metabolism slows along with it. They move less, eat less, and spend more time near the warmer bottom layers rather than cruising the surface. This is a normal seasonal adjustment, not illness. The danger rarely comes from the cold itself; it comes from shallow water that can freeze through, low oxygen under a sealed-over surface, and feeding fish whose digestion has already shut down.

Temperature ranges at a glance

The following are general reference ranges for pond koi. Actual comfort depends on pond depth, stocking level, oxygen and how gradually the temperature drops:

Water temperatureTypical koi stateFeeding approach
Above ~15 °C (59 °F)Active, normal appetite and swimmingRegular maintenance feed, small portions
~10–15 °C (50–59 °F)Slowing down, appetite reducedSwitch to easily digested wheatgerm-type food, reduce amount
~5–10 °C (41–50 °F)Largely inactive, resting near the bottomFeed only lightly, or stop if appetite fades
Below ~5 °C (41 °F)Minimal movement, metabolism near its winter lowDo not feed; undigested food fouls the water
TipThe most reliable rule is appetite, not the thermometer alone. If the fish are not rising to take food, stop feeding rather than forcing meals into cold water. See the cold-water feeding guide for the seasonal transition.

When cold becomes a real risk

Koi can overwinter under ice in a properly sized, deep pond, but several conditions make cold dangerous:

  • Shallow water: A thin pond can chill quickly or freeze toward the bottom, leaving no stable refuge layer. Depth matters more than any single air-temperature reading.
  • A fully sealed-over surface: If ice covers the whole pond and blocks gas exchange, oxygen can drop and waste gases build up beneath it.
  • Low oxygen: Cold water holds more oxygen, but a heavily stocked pond, decaying leaves or an inactive filter can still leave fish short of breath.
  • Sudden temperature swings: A rapid chill after a mild spell stresses fish more than a slow, steady cooling.

Ice on the surface: what is safe

Partial ice on the surface is usually fine. The key is keeping an open area so gases can exchange and you can still observe the fish. Common practices include leaving a pump outlet or waterfall moving near the surface, using a pond heater or de-icer to maintain a breathing hole, and keeping an eye on whether the ice is thickening across the whole pond. Never smash ice hard enough to send shock waves through the water, and do not assume a thick white surface means the water beneath is safe to leave sealed.

WarningIf the pond is shallow, heavily stocked, or has a history of winter losses, plan depth and aeration before the first freeze rather than reacting to an iced-over surface. Read about winter pond depth and heaters vs de-icers ahead of time.

Warning signs in cold water

Reduced movement and appetite are expected in winter, but the following are not normal and should be checked against water quality and oxygen:

  • Fish gasping or hanging near a small open hole at the surface
  • Listless fish that do not move away when approached
  • Visible sores, clamped fins or unusual skin changes
  • Cloudy or foul-smelling water under the ice
