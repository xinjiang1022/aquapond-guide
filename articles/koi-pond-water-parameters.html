Koi Pond Water Parameters: pH, Ammonia, KH and More Explained

Water Quality~10 min readUpdated Sep 12, 2026

Koi are hardy fish, but only within a fairly narrow range of water chemistry. Every pond problem — from gasping fish to green water — traces back to a parameter out of line. Here is what each test means, the numbers to aim for, and how to fix the ones that matter most.

The quick reference table

Print this out and tape it next to your test kit. These are the ranges most koi keepers and pond professionals target:

Ammonia, nitrite and nitrate — the big three

These three are linked by the nitrogen cycle. Fish waste produces ammonia, which is toxic even at very low levels. Beneficial bacteria convert it to nitrite (still toxic), then to nitrate (much safer).

  • Ammonia above 0: the filter isn’t mature yet, or something is producing waste faster than bacteria can handle — overfeeding, a dead fish, or too many fish. Do 20–30% water changes daily until it reads zero, and cut feeding.
  • Nitrite above 0: the cycle is mid-way. Add aquarium salt at 0.1–0.3% to protect fish from nitrite poisoning, and keep up small water changes.
  • Nitrate creeping up: normal and expected — nitrate is the end of the cycle. The only real removal is water changes and plants. If nitrate stays high, you are either under-filtered, overstocked or not changing enough water.

pH and KH: the buffering pair

KH (carbonate hardness) is the most underrated parameter in pond keeping. It acts as a buffer that stops pH from crashing. When KH drops below ~60 ppm, pH can swing wildly — and a pH crash is one of the most common sudden causes of fish death in established ponds.

  • If KH is low, add baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) in small doses: roughly 1 tablespoon per 100 gallons (≈15 g per 380 L) to raise KH by ~10 ppm. Retest after a few hours.
  • Rainwater is soft and acidic — ponds in rainy regions need KH monitoring most.
  • Never chase pH with chemicals; stabilize KH first and pH usually follows.
TipConsistency beats perfection. Koi adapt to pH 7.0 or 8.5 equally well — what they cannot survive is a rapid swing. A stable 7.5 is far better than an "ideal" 8.0 that moves 0.5 every day.

Temperature and oxygen work together

Warm water holds less oxygen, and fish need more oxygen when it is warm. This is why summer heatwaves cause koi to gasp at the surface even when everything else looks fine.

  • In summer: run aeration 24/7, add shade (lilies, floating plants), and avoid feeding in the hottest part of the day.
  • In winter: koi slow down below ~10 °C and stop feeding below ~8 °C. Read our Winter Koi Pond Care guide for the full routine.
  • A waterfall or air stone adds oxygen; a still, plant-choked pond surface is an oxygen problem waiting to happen.

How often should you test?

SituationTesting frequency
New pond, first 2 monthsAmmonia + nitrite every 2–3 days
Established pond, summerpH + ammonia + nitrite weekly; KH monthly
After adding fish, medication or heavy rainTest daily for a few days
Fish behaving strangelyTest everything immediately — water quality first, disease second
WarningWhen a fish looks sick, 80% of the time the real problem is water quality, not disease. Test before you treat. Adding medication to already-poor water usually makes things worse.
