Koi Lost After Winter: What Happened?

Fish Health~7 min readUpdated Nov 16, 2026

There is no easy way to say it: opening the pond in spring and counting fewer koi is one of the hardest moments in pond keeping. It helps, though, to understand the usual culprits so you are not guessing next autumn. Most winter losses trace back to a handful of causes rather than bad luck. (See how to prepare in winter pond care.)

Why koi may not survive winter

Koi are cold-tolerant, but cold still stresses them. Their metabolism slows, they stop eating, and they rely on stored reserves and stable water conditions all the way until spring. When something in that setup fails, losses often show up only when the pond thaws.

The most common causes

CauseWhat happens
Low oxygen under iceA fully iced-over pond can lose oxygen exchange as fish and waste keep using it up
Weak or already-stressed fishFish that entered winter thin or recovering from illness are less likely to last
Feeding too late in autumnCold-water digestion is poor; food left undigested can cause stress (see cold-water feeding)
Poor water quality going into winterSludge and leftover waste keep decomposing slowly under the ice
ImportantThis is general information, not a diagnosis. If you lose fish repeatedly or see unusual symptoms, involve a vet or specialist who can examine the fish.

What to do in spring

When the pond thaws, avoid a massive water change at once. Test the water, keep the filter running gently, and let the pond warm up gradually before resuming feeding (see spring startup). Clean any sludge that built up over winter rather than stirring it all in one go. The goal is a calm, stable restart, not a shock.

How to reduce winter losses next year

Most of the work happens before winter arrives. Give the pond a gentle clean of sludge in autumn, switch to cold-water feeding and stop feeding once the water stays cold, and keep some form of aeration or surface opening running under the ice so oxygen can keep exchanging (see winter care). Fish that go into winter healthy, with clean water and open gas exchange, are far more likely to make it through.
