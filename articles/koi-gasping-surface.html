Why Koi Gasp at the Surface: Low Oxygen and What to Check

Fish Health~6 min readUpdated Nov 25, 2026

Koi that hang at the surface with their mouths open, gulping at the air-water boundary, are usually sending a stress signal. Low dissolved oxygen is the first suspect, but warm water, overcrowding, a clogged filter, leftover feed and rising ammonia can produce the same behavior. The right response is to observe calmly, run a short checklist from the most likely causes, and avoid dumping in treatments before you know what you are dealing with.

The short answer

If most of your fish are gathered near the surface, especially near a waterfall or moving outlet, low oxygen is the most common explanation. This often shows up on warm nights, after heavy feeding, or when the pump or air supply has stopped. Gasping can also signal poor water quality — particularly ammonia or nitrite — or illness in individual fish. A fish that otherwise feeds and swims normally may simply be grazing; persistent gulping, especially from several fish, deserves attention.

RememberSurface gulping is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Fish will gasp for oxygen in several different situations, and a chemical added to “treat the gasping” will not fix a pump that has stopped or a pond stocked beyond its limits.

What surface gasping looks like

Watch the fish before you react. Stress behavior includes lingering just under the surface with the mouth repeatedly breaking the water, gathering near the waterfall or skimmer, or ignoring food that normally attracts them. Compare this with normal activity: koi commonly rise to feed, poke at floating food or patrol near plants, so a brief surface visit is not alarming. Persistent gulping across several fish, with reduced feeding or listlessness, is the pattern to take seriously.

Common causes at a glance

The following causes overlap, and more than one can be present at the same time. Treat this table as a starting point for your own observation rather than a final diagnosis.

A calm step-by-step check

1. Confirm the aeration is actually running

Start with the equipment that supplies oxygen. Make sure the pump is on and moving water through the filter and waterfall, that the air pump (if you have one) is working, and that no outlet or nozzle is blocked or buried. A tripped circuit, clogged impeller or split airline can quietly remove aeration overnight. Aeration options are compared in the pond aeration guide.

2. Look at temperature and weather

Warm water holds less oxygen, and a hot, still day can push a loaded pond toward its limit. Heat also speeds up decomposition, so sludge and leftover food consume oxygen faster. Check whether the pond has enough shade in the hottest part of the day — see the guide to shading a pond naturally and floating plants for shade.

3. Test the water

Use a reliable test kit or strip to check ammonia, nitrite, pH and temperature. Persistent gasping that does not improve once aeration is restored often points to ammonia or nitrite from overfeeding, an immature cycle or a clogged filter. Reading the signs is covered in pond water testing and the nitrogen cycle guide.

4. Review stocking and feeding

A pond holding too many large fish will keep competing for oxygen, especially in summer. Compare your load with the guidance in how many koi a pond can hold. Also cut back on feeding: uneaten food decays, consumes oxygen and pushes ammonia up. Feed lightly in warm weather according to the timing covered in when to feed koi.

5. Check the fish themselves

If only one or two fish are gasping while the rest look normal, illness or gill irritation becomes more likely. Look for flashing or rubbing, drooping fins, ulcers, excess slime, clamped fins or a change in color. Other behavior signals are covered alongside this one in why koi flash and rub, and the main health problems are listed in common koi diseases.

What to do right now

While you check, keep the waterfall or air supply running and avoid stirring up bottom sludge that could release more waste. Stop feeding until the fish settle and the water has been checked. If readings are poor, a partial water change with dechlorinated, temperature-matched water can relieve pressure; change only a modest portion, since a sudden water swing itself stresses fish. Safe topping-up and replacement are explained in the pond water change guide and dechlorinating tap water.

WarningDo not add medications, salt treatments or algaecides on top of a gasping episode without a clear cause. Many treatments add further stress to fish that are already short of oxygen, and they can mask the real problem.

When to call a specialist

Seek advice from a reputable aquatic specialist or veterinarian if gasping continues after aeration and equipment are confirmed working, if several fish show other symptoms at once, if you see reddened or swollen gills, or if fish stop feeding and begin resting on the bottom. Gill problems in particular are hard to assess without a close examination.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Treating the symptom first: A dose of medication cannot replace a stopped pump, a clogged filter or an overloaded pond.
  • Large, sudden water changes: A dramatic water swing can stress already vulnerable fish; change water in modest, matched portions.
  • Keeping on feeding: Leftover feed worsens oxygen demand and ammonia while fish are already struggling.
  • Reading one fish’s surface visit as an emergency: Separate normal feeding or exploring from persistent gulping across several fish.
