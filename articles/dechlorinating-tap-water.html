How to Dechlorinate Tap Water for Koi Ponds

Water Quality~9 min readUpdated Nov 3, 2026

Fresh tap water looks harmless, but the chemicals your water company adds to keep it safe for people can burn koi gills and wipe out the bacteria in your filter — during a routine water change. The fix is simple once you know whether you are dealing with chlorine or the far more stubborn chloramine.

Why tap water is dangerous for koi

Municipal water is disinfected on purpose, and the disinfectant does not stop being disinfectant the moment it leaves your hose. Two chemicals matter for pond keeping:

  • Chlorine — the familiar smell. It evaporates relatively quickly when water is left to stand or agitated.
  • Chloramine — chlorine bonded to ammonia. It is increasingly common because it lasts longer in pipes, but it does not off-gas by waiting. It is also harder on fish and on filter bacteria.

Both irritate the delicate gill membranes and can kill the beneficial bacteria doing work in your biological filter — which is exactly why a routine water change can suddenly send ammonia and nitrite climbing. See The Nitrogen Cycle to understand what is at risk.

WarningNever dump untreated tap water straight into a pond, even a small top-up. A large, cold, chlorinated addition in one go is a double shock — chemical and temperature. Treat and match the temperature first.

How do I know if my water has chloramine?

Call your local water supplier and ask whether they use chlorine or chloramine, or pick up an inexpensive test kit that checks for chloramine directly. This matters because the old trick of "leave the water out overnight" works for chlorine and does almost nothing for chloramine.

TipA pond-rated water conditioner is the reliable default: it works instantly, handles both chlorine and chloramine, and removes the guesswork. Add it to the incoming water as it fills, following the dose on the bottle — under-dosing is a common mistake with large water changes.

A safe water-change routine

  • Match temperature. Let the new water sit until it is within a few degrees of the pond before adding it.
  • Treat as you fill. Dose the conditioner into the stream of incoming water, not after the fact.
  • Go gradual. Add the new water over time rather than all at once; see How Often Should You Change Pond Water? for sensible volumes.
  • Re-test after a big change. Check ammonia and nitrite the next day — especially if your filter was already under strain.

Common dechlorinating mistakes

Two errors come up again and again. The first is treating only the "obvious" water change and ignoring top-ups — every litre of tap water you add matters, even a small evaporation refill. The second is under-dosing conditioner when the water arrives cold; cold water holds chlorine differently, and a half-strength dose may leave enough behind to stress the gills. When in doubt, dose slightly above the bottle guidance for the volume you actually added, not the pond total.

If you do everything right and still see stressed fish after a change, the problem is usually not the water chemistry but the filter — test your parameters with our weekly testing routine and read Koi Pond Water Parameters.
