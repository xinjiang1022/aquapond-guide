Why Koi Rest on the Pond Bottom: Normal Resting vs a Stress Signal

Koi Care~6 min readUpdated Nov 27, 2026

A koi that hovers low or settles near the bottom is not automatically sick. Fish rest, seek cooler water, save energy in cold weather and hide when they feel exposed. The difference between a normal low-level rest and a warning sign comes down to the pattern: when it happens, whether the fish reacts to feeding, and what the rest of the pond and water are doing. This guide helps you read that behavior before jumping to conclusions.

The short answer

Single fish that sit near the bottom briefly, respond to food, and swim up normally are often resting or conserving energy. Several fish hanging low, a fish that stays down all day, or bottom-resting alongside poor appetite, clamped fins, flashing, surface gasping or cloudy water points toward stress, water quality trouble or illness. Start with observation and a water test rather than reaching for treatment.

What to watchNote the time of day, water temperature, who is resting, and whether the fish feeds when approached. A written pattern over a day or two usually reveals whether this is a habit or a problem.

Why koi stay near the bottom

The causes range from completely normal to needing action. Use the table to match the behavior you are seeing to the most likely explanation.

A step-by-step observation check

1. Watch the behavior pattern

Spend quiet time at the pond across morning, afternoon and evening. Healthy rest is intermittent: the fish settles for a while, then moves, feeds or explores. A fish that remains on the bottom continuously, refuses to rise, or lets itself be approached too closely is showing a less reassuring pattern. Nighttime settling is explained alongside sleep in the guide to how koi sleep and rest. Persistent withdrawal is covered in why koi hide.

2. Check temperature and season

Cold water slows a koi's metabolism and makes fish less active, so a quiet, bottom-hugging group in winter is often normal rather than sick. Temperature limits and cool-weather behavior are covered in the koi cold-temperature guide, and seasonal habits in winter pond care. Fish that have struggled after a cold season are discussed in koi lost after winter.

3. Test the water

Test ammonia, nitrite, pH and temperature, and confirm the filter, pump and aeration are working. Poor water or low oxygen can push fish to stay low or, conversely, up to gasp at the surface; oxygen and gulping are covered in the surface-gasping guide. Readings and target ranges are in the water parameters guide, and routine testing in the pond testing routine. Fish affected after a change are addressed in fish lost after a water change.

4. Test the feeding response

Offer a small amount of their usual food. A fish that leaves the bottom to feed and feeds normally is generally doing better than one that stays down while food passes. Refusal that lasts more than a day or two, or that pairs with weight loss, deserves attention; appetite problems are covered in why koi stop eating. Do not overfeed a pond of slow, inactive fish.

Over several visits, notice how much time the fish spends cruising the middle and upper water compared with sitting low. A group that rises to patrol and feed, then settles again, shows a healthier balance than one that stays pressed to the base the whole time. That contrast matters more than a single glance at the pond at dusk.

5. Inspect the fish for health signs

Look for flashing and rubbing, clamped or drooping fins, ulcers, red patches, excess slime, uneven color or bulging eyes. Main conditions to compare against are listed in common koi diseases. If one fish alone is weak while the rest feed and swim normally, focus the check on that individual rather than assuming a pond-wide emergency.

When bottom-resting is a warning

Treat low-level resting as a problem worth acting on when the fish stays down for most of the day, will not feed, appears thin or weak, cannot hold its balance, develops fins or body symptoms, or when several fish show the same behavior together. In those cases, correct any water issue you find and seek advice from a reputable aquatic specialist or veterinarian if the pattern continues.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Treating every low rest as illness: Resting, cold-water sluggishness and nighttime settling are normal; watch the pattern before acting.
  • Skipping the water test: Poor chemistry or low oxygen often drives behavior changes and should be ruled out first.
  • Reaching for medication early: Medication adds stress and cannot fix bad water or a simple disturbance.
  • Overlooking recent events: A new fish, water change, predator or loud disturbance can send normally active koi into hiding.
