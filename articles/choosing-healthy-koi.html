How to Choose Healthy Koi: A Buyer’s Checklist

Fish Health~9 min readUpdated Sep 14, 2026

The cheapest koi is the one you never have to treat. Most pond disease outbreaks start with a single sick newcomer — which is why choosing healthy fish and quarantining them is the highest-value skill in koi keeping. This checklist takes you from the dealer’s tank to a safe introduction.

Start with the seller, not the fish

A good seller makes healthy koi much easier to find:

  • Water quality at the shop: holding tanks should look clear and smell clean. Cloudy, green or ammonia-smelling tanks are a red flag for the whole batch.
  • Stocking density: crowded tanks stress fish and spread parasites. A dealer who keeps too many fish in one tank is not a dealer you should trust.
  • Quarantine visibly used: ask whether new arrivals are quarantined. Reputable dealers have separate holding systems and can tell you how long a fish has been in their care.
  • Reputation: koi forums and local koi clubs quickly reveal which dealers have healthy stock and honest practices.

The 8-point body check

Once you are looking at a candidate fish, work through these points before you commit:

CheckWhat healthy looks likeWhat to avoid
1. EyesClear, bright, both eyes levelSunken or cloudy eyes
2. FinsFull, intact, held open while swimmingRagged, milky or red-streaked fins; clamped fins
3. Skin & scalesSmooth, glossy, no spots or raised patchesWhite grains (ich), cottony tufts, ulcers, red streaks, raised pinecone scales
4. GillsGill covers move evenly; gill flaps not swollenOne gill clamped, rapid or laboured breathing
5. SwimmingSteady, upright, alertFlashing (rubbing), listing to one side, gasping at surface
6. AppetiteComes to food eagerlyIgnores food, hangs at the back
7. Body shapeBroad, deep, proportional bodyPinched belly, concave flanks, bloated look
8. BehaviourSocial, swimming with the groupIsolating itself in a corner or at the bottom

Red flags to walk away from

Any single one of these is a reason to pass on a fish — even at a bargain price:

  • Flashing or rubbing against tank walls (parasite sign)
  • Gasping at the surface in well-aerated water (gill or water-quality problem)
  • White salt-like grains on the body (ich)
  • Cotton-wool patches (fungus, usually over a wound)
  • Red ulcers, streaks or open sores (bacterial infection)
  • Sunken eyes or a thin, pinched body (long-term poor condition)
  • Any fish in the tank showing these signs — even if your candidate looks fine

For the diseases behind these signs, see 15 Common Koi Diseases: Symptoms and First Steps.

Quarantine: the step almost everyone skips

You can buy the healthiest-looking koi in the world and still bring parasites into your pond — many pathogens are invisible for days or weeks. Quarantine is what protects the fish you already own:

  1. Set up a quarantine tank (a large tub or stock tank works) with its own filter, aeration and a lid/net — at least 2–4 weeks of isolation.
  2. Run the quarantine tank for a few days first so it is stable before the fish arrives.
  3. Observe daily: appetite, swimming, breathing, skin. Quarantine is not a waiting room — it is a monitoring period.
  4. Never move equipment between tanks without cleaning — nets, buckets and hands carry pathogens.
  5. Introduce to the pond gently: float/equalise temperature, then release. Do not add new fish during cold snaps or when the pond itself is stressed.
WarningIf you already have fish, quarantine is non-negotiable. One carrier fish can infect an entire pond within days. The 2–4 week wait is cheap insurance compared with treating a full outbreak.

Before you bring koi home

  • Make sure your pond is cycled and water tests are zeroed out — see the nitrogen cycle and the beginner’s guide.
  • Know your target stocking level and stick to it — stocking density.
  • Transport koi in a suitable insulated bag or container with oxygen; keep the trip short and the container shaded.
RememberPrice reflects genetics, pattern and size — not health. A healthy $20 koi is a better purchase than a sick $200 one. Health first, pattern second.
