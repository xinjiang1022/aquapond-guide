Koi Pond UV Clarifier: Do You Really Need One?

Equipment~9 min readUpdated Nov 2, 2026

A UV clarifier is the fastest way to turn pea-soup green water clear — and also the easiest piece of pond equipment to buy for the wrong reason. Before you spend the money, understand what UV light can and cannot do, how to size it honestly, and when the real fix is not a lamp at all.

What a UV clarifier actually does

A UV clarifier is a sealed tube through which pond water flows, passing a germicidal ultraviolet lamp. The light damages the DNA of single-celled algae floating in the water, so they die and clump into particles your filter can trap. It is a clarifier, not a filter: it does not remove anything from the pond by itself — it just makes the algae easier for your mechanical filtration to catch.

That distinction explains its biggest limit: UV only works on green water (free-floating algae). It does nothing for string algae growing on the liner, and it does not treat most parasites or bacteria because the light only touches the water passing through the unit, not the fish.

Common misunderstandingUV units are not disease treatments. They kill some free-swimming organisms, but relying on one to protect koi from parasites is a mistake — see our quarantine guide for what actually keeps disease out of a pond.

Green water vs string algae: two different problems

Green water (free-floating algae)String algae (attached growth)
AppearanceWater turns cloudy, pea-soup greenGreen mats, threads and tufts on walls and rocks
CauseNutrients + sunlight in the water columnNutrients + sunlight on surfaces
Does UV help?Yes — this is its jobNo — it misses what is attached
Typical fixUV + filtration + shadingManual removal + nutrient control

If your pond has string algae instead of green water, a UV clarifier will not solve it — spend the money on removal and nutrient control instead. We break the two apart in String Algae vs Green Water: Spot the Difference.

Sizing a UV unit: watts, flow and honesty

Manufacturers rate UV units by the pond volume they can handle, but the real constraint is flow rate: the slower water passes the lamp, the more UV dose it receives. The typical sizing guidance below is a starting point, not a law — always match the unit to your pump’s flow, not the other way around.

Pond volumeTypical clarifier wattage (guide)Key sizing rule
Under 1,000 gallons9–18 WMatch the unit’s rated flow to your pump
1,000–2,500 gallons25–36 WOversize slightly if the pond gets heavy sun
2,500–5,000 gallons40–55 WConsider two units or a larger one at 2× flow
5,000+ gallons80 W or multiple unitsSplit flow across parallel units if needed
TipWhen in doubt, choose a unit rated for roughly 1.5–2× your pond volume. An undersized UV with a strong pump is the most common reason “UV doesn’t work” — the water simply rushes past the lamp too fast to be zapped.

Where and how to install it

  • After the pump, before the filter. The pump pushes water through the UV, then into the mechanical filter, which catches the clumped algae. Placing it after the filter wastes most of the light on already-clean water.
  • Off the pond floor. Mount the unit where it is easy to service, with unions on both sides so you can pull it out for cleaning.
  • Keep the glass clean. A film of biofilm on the quartz sleeve blocks UV light. Wipe the sleeve a couple of times a season — a dirty sleeve is the #2 reason UV units underperform.
  • Replace the lamp on schedule. UV output fades with hours of use. Manufacturers typically suggest replacing the bulb every 8,000–12,000 running hours or roughly each season — even if the lamp still glows, it may no longer be effective.

When you do NOT need a UV clarifier

  • Your pond stays clear. If green water has never been a problem, a UV unit is a maintenance item with nothing to do. Many healthy ponds run fine without one.
  • The real cause is overfeeding or under-filtration. A UV will hide the symptom while ammonia and nutrients keep climbing. Fix the nutrient load first: feeding discipline, filter capacity and water changes are the long-term answer.
  • You keep plants heavily shaded and stocked in balance. Ponds with good plant cover and moderate stocking often suppress algae naturally.

Think of UV as a tool for managing green water, not a substitute for filtration. If your pond is also struggling with murky, brown or cloudy water, start with our Murky Pond Water guide and make sure the pump is sized correctly (How to Choose the Right Pond Pump).
